Open-source keyboard / 2025

Pulp.

A minimal, low-profile split ortholinear keyboard with 40 keys.

A small keyboard, deliberately

Less reach.
More intention.

Pulp began with a simple desire: make a split keyboard that feels as clean as it is capable. Split layouts are a revelation ergonomically, but I did not want that comfort to come with visual clutter or unnecessary complexity.

I took cues from the Totem’s restraint and the Corne’s familiar thumb cluster, then made the design my own: a compact, slightly staggered layout, low-profile Choc switches, and just forty keys. Each half carries a tiny OLED display and a low-profile knob, so the board remains expressive without becoming busy.

Designing
the quiet details.

The goal stayed constant throughout: an ergonomic board with a calm silhouette. Getting there involved a few useful detours.

01 / Research

Finding the balance

I started by studying the keyboards I kept returning to. The Totem made a case for minimalism, while the Corne’s thumb cluster felt like a strong foundation. That comparison set the direction: low profile was non-negotiable, but the layout still needed enough contour and reachability to feel intentional in use.

02 / Schematic

One schematic, two halves

The electronics came together around nice!nano v2 controllers, hot-swappable Choc switches, diodes, OLEDs, and encoders. I designed both sides around the same schematic from the beginning, with a flippable PCB in mind—one board design instead of separate left and right orders.

03 / PCB

Letting the layout tool help

After spending too long wrestling with grids, splay, and mirrored placement in KiCad, I switched to Ergogen for the layout work. It was the right pivot: the component positions became easier to iterate on, and the flippable-board constraint stopped dominating every decision. Back in KiCad, I refined the placement, added 3D models, and prepared the board for routing.

04 / Enclosure

Giving the electronics a home

With the board taking shape, I moved into Onshape to assemble the PCB and components, then designed the case, bottom plate, and screw locations around them. The final stretch was about resolution: finishing the bill of materials, rendering the form in Blender, and routing the traces that turn the idea into a buildable keyboard.
